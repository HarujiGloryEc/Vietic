### Pronunciation

Japanese has roughly **100–120 basic sound units (morae)**, while Vietnamese has approximately **4,000–6,000 syllables, including tones**.<br/>
Therefore, Vietnamese speakers can pronounce almost all Japanese sounds.<br/>
Only a few combinations, such as **ryo (りょ)** and **kya (きゃ)**, are unfamiliar to Vietnamese speakers.

## Japanese Writing Systems

After adopting Chinese characters, Japan developed **Man'yōgana (万葉仮名)**, using Chinese characters to represent Japanese sounds. However, it was difficult to learn and use.

- **Katakana (カタカナ):** Developed as a simplified writing system, initially used by Buddhist monks and educated men for annotations and official texts.
- **Hiragana (ひらがな):** Developed from cursive Chinese characters. It became popular among women and people excluded from formal Chinese education.
- **Literature:** Some male nobles even wrote poetry and literature under female identities to use Hiragana.

### Modern Usage

- **Hiragana:** Native Japanese words and grammatical elements.
- **Katakana:** Foreign loanwords and non-Japanese names.
- **Kanji:** Chinese-derived characters used to represent meanings in Japanese words and documents.

### Hiragana (ひらがな)

**Learning method:**
- Write each Hiragana character **50 times a day for 3–5 days** on A4 paper.
- Pronounce each character out loud while writing.
- After 3–5 days, you should be familiar with all Hiragana characters.

**Pronunciation Tips for Vietnamese Learners:**

- **Ryo (りょ):** Start with **R** and end with **yo**. If you cannot pronounce it correctly at first, just say "ryo". This is your second foreign language, so don't worry too much as long as people understand you. :D
- **"Nhật không có cu, chỉ có cư."**: Japanese **く (ku)** sounds closer to Vietnamese "cư".
- **R/L:** Japanese has no separate English-style R and L sounds. You can initially approximate **ra, re, ro, ru, ri** as Vietnamese **la, le, lo, lư, li**.
- **Ryo (りょ):** Since Japanese has no separate English-style R sound, you can pronounce it as Vietnamese **"rô"**. There is no identical sound in Japanese, so they can still understand you. :D
- **Ryu (りゅ):** Means **dragon (竜 / 龍)** as Vietnamese **"riêu"**. :D
- **Kyu (きゅ):** like Vietnamese "niêu", and the same approach applies to Nyu (にゅ), Gyu (ぎゅ), Chū (ちゅ), and other similar Japanese sounds.
- **Gya (ぎゃ), Kya (きゃ), etc.:** If you cannot pronounce them correctly, just split the sounds: **gy-a, ky-a**, etc. :D
- **V/B:** Japanese traditionally uses **B** sounds for foreign **V** sounds. For example, "Victoria" becomes **Bikutoria (ビクトリア)**. Think of Vietnamese **"vĩnh trú" → "bĩnh trú"** as a pronunciation trick.

### Katakana (カタカナ)

**Learning method:**

Just learn and remember Katakana. If you have enough energy, practice writing it as you did with Hiragana. Otherwise, just memorize it.

The Japanese writing system and Katakana rules are complex.<br/>
To keep things simple, just memorize Katakana. You will understand more as you continue learning.

**Loanwords:**

Japanese borrows many words from Western languages.

- **パン (pan):** Bread, from Portuguese *pão*.
- **アルバイト (arubaito):** Part-time job, from German *Arbeit* (work). Japanese students who studied in Germany during the Meiji era encountered this word.
- **ドイツ (Doitsu):** Germany, from German *Deutsch*.
- **英語 (えいご, eigo):** Originally **英吉利語 (えいぎりすご)**, from Chinese, meaning "English language." But the Chinese were also lazy, so they shortened it to the first and last characters: **英 + 語 = 英語**. Because of **Tứ Quốc Đồng Văn (四國同文)**, Japan, Korea, and Vietnam followed the same shortened form: Japanese **英語 (えいご)**, Korean **영어 (英語, yeongeo)**, and Vietnamese **tiếng Anh**. **英** represents England/Britain, while **語** means language.

**Tip:** All four nations are lazy! They shorten loanwords, like "biz" for "business". Remember this to recognize similar words in Japanese.

**Wasei-eigo (和製英語):**

Modern Japanese also creates expressions using English words, but with Japanese pronunciation or different meanings.

- **リボ払い (ribo-barai):** Revolving payment, commonly used in banking. リボ is shortened from *revolving*.
- **チャーム (chāmu):** Charm.
- **ストレス (sutoresu):** Stress.

Sometimes Japanese speakers use English-derived words instead of native Japanese expressions. However, the borrowed words may not carry exactly the same feelings or nuances as the originals.

For example:
- **Elegant:** Refined beauty, grace, and good taste.
- **Dignity:** Similar to Vietnamese **nhân phẩm**, but not exactly the same in cultural meaning. English *dignity* emphasizes an individual's personal worth, while Vietnamese *nhân phẩm*, in your comparison, is more closely connected to family/clan values and reputation.

## Kanji (漢字)

### Western vs. Eastern Civilizations

The Roman Empire and Han Dynasty emerged around the same period.<br/>
In the West, the Roman state and its centralized government survived for centuries, with the Eastern Roman Empire (Byzantine Empire) lasting until the late medieval period.

This helped shape modern Europe, with its rich and diverse cultures.<br/>
Although European nations share common roots, they developed different ideas and became more open to accepting or tolerating new ones.

Meanwhile, **Qin Shi Huang (秦始皇)** was China's first emperor, unified China and went through many dynasties, but **Hanzi (漢字)** remained largely the same, with some changes.<br/>
The ideas associated with those characters were passed down through generations, influencing learners in neighboring countries.

Knowledge accumulated over centuries, building upon earlier ideas.<br/>
That is why Westerners often see Asians as intelligent. But I think **we are not really smarter; we simply stack knowledge from previous generations**.

Western cultures tend to explore new ideas, while Asian cultures tend to preserve and build upon existing knowledge.<br/>
That is also why, in my view, Westerners enjoy traveling and exploring, while Asians prefer staying in one place.
They need to enrich their dictionary, we don't. In the end, we knew the result. <br/>
We are using English as international language.

### The Second Reason Not to Follow Confucianism

As mentioned in **Western vs. Eastern Civilizations**, Eastern societies shared similar ideas and Chinese characters.<br/>
Instead of inventing new ideas, scholars often studied Kanji (Hanzi), tried to understand previous generations' thinking, and repeated it. This limited innovation and the development of new knowledge.

Look at Korea's **Gwageo (科擧)** examination system. Scholars used their knowledge to create barriers to education, making it difficult for ordinary people to learn.

Even after **King Sejong** created Hangul, the educated elite continued to favor Chinese characters.<br/>
During the Japanese occupation, when Korean language and culture were suppressed, Hangul eventually became a symbol of Korean identity.

In my view, those scholars were not the great people we often imagine. They were **worms of the nation**, protecting their social status rather than helping ordinary people.

Vietnam had similar problems with **Nho scholars (nhà Nho)**. They widened the gap between social classes and restricted access to education.

Without **Alexandre de Rhodes** and **Trương Vĩnh Ký**, who helped develop and popularize **Chữ Quốc Ngữ**, we might still be relying on Chinese characters.

Hangul and Quốc Ngữ use phonetic writing systems, but Chinese-derived vocabulary remains deeply embedded in both languages.

For example:
- **Bạn có sử dụng đồ điện tử ở nhà không?** — *Sử dụng* and *điện tử* are Sino-Vietnamese words.
- **Cơ quan công**, **trường học**, **quốc gia** — These also contain Chinese-derived vocabulary.

Japanese and Korean have similar situations.

**Therefore, when learning Japanese, you can recognize the shared Chinese-character roots connecting Vietnamese, Japanese, and Korean—and vice versa.**

### Kanji Writing Rules

**For each character:** Write strokes from **top to bottom, left to right**.

**For traditional books:** Write columns from **top to bottom, right to left**.<br/>
This comes from ancient Chinese writing traditions, when people wrote on bamboo slips that were tied together and rolled into scrolls.

| Style | Chinese | Japanese | Description |
|---|---|---|---|

Why does this matter? Some Kanji use **Lệ thư**, while others use **Khải thư**. When searching for a character, you may find **very different forms**, sometimes looking like **completely different Kanji**. There are also handwritten styles, but I won't cover them here.

## Japanese Culture

### Don't Forget Punctuality

Always arrive **5–10 minutes early**, or you will be considered irresponsible.<br/>
If you have an issue and cannot arrive on time, **inform them in advance**, or you will be in serious trouble.<br/>
This also applies to **work and project deadlines**.

### Bowing

When bowing, **men** usually keep both hands straight at their sides, against their thighs.<br/>
**Women** usually place both hands together in front of their lower body before bowing.

- **15° (Eshaku / 会釈):** Casual greeting.
- **30° (Keirei / 敬礼):** Respectful bow.
- **45° (Saikeirei / 最敬礼):** Deep respect or sincere apology.
- **90°:** Extremely deep bow for a serious apology or request.
- **Dogeza (土下座):** Kneel on the floor, lower your upper body, and place both hands on the ground.<br/>
Bow until your forehead nearly touches or touches the floor.<br/>
Used for an extreme apology or desperate request, when you throw away your last bit of ego and dignity.

**Koreans**
Similar to Japanese bowing, Koreans use different bow depths to show respect.<br/>
For everyday standing bows, both men and women can follow the same basic rules without worrying too much about hand placement.

### Smooth Communication and Apologies

You will notice that Japanese people apologize frequently and are always ready to do so.<br/>
Apologizing does not mean losing your position, title, or money. All you lose is a little worthless ego.

You may even see coworkers apologize on Monday before starting work.<br/>
You will also hear **arigatō (ありがとう)** very often.

So, learn these two important phrases: **sumimasen (すみません)** and **arigatō (ありがとう)**.<br/>
They help you **read the room**, understand the situation, and communicate and work smoothly with others.

They also help you avoid being seen as cold or unfriendly.
### Hōrensō (報連相)

**Hōrensō** is very important. It prevents you from leaving your boss in the dark and helps them prepare for the worst situations.<br/>
You must learn Hōrensō later in the soft skills section. Without it, you will soon be in trouble when working with Japanese customers.

### Wabi-Sabi (侘び寂び)

Influenced by **Buddhism**, Wabi-Sabi teaches us that everything is impermanent and will eventually come to an end.<br/>
Therefore, we should cherish what we have now.

This idea is reflected in **Hanami (花見)**, when Japanese people admire cherry blossoms because their beauty lasts only a short time.

It also influences Japanese aesthetics, including simplicity, natural materials, and the appreciation of imperfections.

**White clothing and subtle patterns** are also associated with Japanese ideas of simplicity and purity.

### Wa–Kei–Sei–Jaku (和敬清寂)

The four principles of the Japanese tea ceremony: **Harmony, Respect, Purity, and Tranquility**.<br/>
Derived from **Buddhist philosophy**, especially Zen Buddhism, these principles are traditionally associated with **Sen no Rikyū (千利休, 1522–1591)**, who shaped the Japanese tea ceremony during the Sengoku period.
